The limits of traditional cosmological inference

HSC cosmic shear power spectrum
HSC Y1 constraints on \((S_8, \Omega_m)\)

Hikage et al. 2018

  • Measure the ellipticity \(\epsilon = \epsilon_i + \gamma\) of all galaxies
    \(\Longrightarrow\) Noisy tracer of the weak lensing shear \(\gamma\)
  • Compute summary statistics based on 2pt functions,
    e.g. the power spectrum
  • Run an MCMC to recover a posterior on model parameters, using an analytic likelihood $$ p(\theta | x ) \propto \underbrace{p(x | \theta)}_{\mathrm{likelihood}} \ \underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\mathrm{prior}}$$

Main limitation: the need for an explicit likelihood

We can only compute from theory the likelihood for simple summary statistics and on large scales \(\Longrightarrow\) We are dismissing a significant fraction of the information!

Beyond conventional analyses

Full-field simulation-based inference

  • Instead of trying to analytically evaluate the likelihood of sub-optimal summary statistics, let us build a forward model of the full observables.
    \(\Longrightarrow\) The simulator becomes the physical model.
  • Each component of the model is now tractable, but at the cost of a large number of latent variables.

Benefits of a forward modeling approach

  • Fully exploits the information content of the data (aka “full field inference”).
  • Easy to incorporate systematic effects.
  • Easy to combine multiple cosmological probes by joint simulations.

\(\Longrightarrow\) These methods demonstrably give access to more information.

Canonical vs. microcanonical MCMC samplers

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (e.g. Neal 2011)

\(\mathcal H(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol p) = U(\boldsymbol q) + \tfrac 1 2 \boldsymbol p^\top M^{-1} \boldsymbol p\)

samples the canonical ensemble

\(\mathrm p_\text{C}(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol p) \propto e^{-\mathcal H(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol p)} \propto \mathrm p(\boldsymbol q)\,\mathcal N(\boldsymbol 0, M)\)

\(\mathcal H(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol p) = \frac {\boldsymbol p^\top M^{-1} \boldsymbol p} {2 m(\boldsymbol q)} - \frac{m(\boldsymbol q)}{2}\,,\quad m=e^{-U/(d-1)}\)

Challenge 1 · Efficient posterior sampling

Canonical MCMC samplers

Recipe to sample from \(\mathrm p \propto e^{-U}\):

  • take a particle with position \(\boldsymbol q\), momentum \(\boldsymbol p\), mass matrix \(M\), and Hamiltonian $$\mathcal H(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol p) = U(\boldsymbol q) + \tfrac 1 2 \boldsymbol p^\top M^{-1} \boldsymbol p$$
  • follow Hamiltonian dynamics during time \(L\) $$\begin{cases} \dot {\boldsymbol q} = \partial_{\boldsymbol p}\mathcal H = M^{-1}\boldsymbol p\\ \dot {\boldsymbol p} = -\partial_{\boldsymbol q}\mathcal H = - \nabla U(\boldsymbol q) \end{cases}$$ and refresh momentum \(\boldsymbol p \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol 0,M)\)
  • usually, perform a Metropolis adjustment
  • this samples the canonical ensemble $$\mathrm p_\text{C}(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol p) \propto e^{-\mathcal H(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol p)} \propto \mathrm p(\boldsymbol q)\,\mathcal N(\boldsymbol 0, M)$$

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (e.g. Neal 2011)

gradient guides the particle toward high-density sets
must average over all energy levels
scales poorly with dimension
Challenge 1 · Efficient posterior sampling

Microcanonical MCMC samplers

Recipe to sample from \(\mathrm p \propto e^{-U}\):

  • take a particle with position \(\boldsymbol q\), momentum \(\boldsymbol p\), mass matrix \(M\), and Hamiltonian $$\mathcal H(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol p) = \frac {\boldsymbol p^\top M^{-1} \boldsymbol p} {2 m(\boldsymbol q)} - \frac{m(\boldsymbol q)}{2}\,,\quad m=e^{-U/(d-1)}$$
  • follow Hamiltonian dynamics during time \(L\) $$\begin{cases} \dot{\boldsymbol q} = M^{-1/2} \boldsymbol u\\ \dot{\boldsymbol u} = -(I - \boldsymbol u \boldsymbol u^\top) M^{-1/2} \nabla U(\boldsymbol q) / (d-1) \end{cases}$$ and refresh \(\boldsymbol u \leftarrow \boldsymbol z/ \lvert \boldsymbol z \rvert\,,\ \boldsymbol z \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol 0,I)\)
  • usually, perform a Metropolis adjustment — let’s try avoiding that
  • this samples the microcanonical / isokinetic ensemble $$\mathrm p_\text{C}(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol u) \propto \delta(\mathcal H(\boldsymbol q, \boldsymbol u)) \propto \mathrm p (\boldsymbol q)\, \delta(|\boldsymbol u| - 1)$$

Microcanonical HMC (Robnik+2022)

gradient guides the particle toward high-density sets

Inverse problems 101

Linear inverse problems

\(\boxed{\,y = \mathbf{A}x + n\,}\)

\(\mathbf{A}\) is known and encodes our physical understanding of the measurement process. \(\Longrightarrow\) When \(\mathbf{A}\) is non-invertible or ill-conditioned, the problem is ill-posed: there is no unique solution \(x\).

Deconvolution

Inverse problems 101

A Bayesian view of the problem

\(y = \mathbf{A}x + n \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p(x \mid y) \propto p(y \mid x)\, p(x)\)

The likelihood \(p(y \mid x)\)

Contains the physics: the instrument, the noise model, the forward operator \(\mathbf{A}\).

The prior \(p(x)\)

Our knowledge about plausible solutions, before looking at the data.

With these in hand we can estimate the maximum a posteriori solution,

\(\hat{x} = \arg\max_x\ \log p(y \mid x) + \log p(x) \;=\; \arg\max_x\ -\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert y - \mathbf{A}x\rVert^2_{\Sigma} + \log p(x)\ \text{ for Gaussian noise,}\)

or aim to sample the full posterior \(p(x \mid y)\), e.g. with MCMC.

How do you choose the prior?

For images, fields, or spectra, \(p(x)\) is only known implicitly through examples. \(\Longrightarrow\) Learn it from data with a deep generative model \(p_\theta(x)\), and use it as a prior.

Reminder: diffusion models as data-driven priors

DataForward SDEPriorReverse SDEData

Song et al. 2021

Training = denoising

Noising the data, \(x_t = x + \sigma_t z\), defines a family of smoothed distributions \(p_t(x_t)\). A denoiser \(d_\theta(x_t, t) \approx \mathbb{E}[x \mid x_t]\) is trained with a simple loss:

\(\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x, t, z}\, \lambda_t \lVert d_\theta(x + \sigma_t z, t) - x\rVert^2\)

The denoiser gives the score (Tweedie)

\(\nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t) = \dfrac{d_\theta(x_t, t) - x_t}{\sigma_t^2}\)

Plugging this score into the reverse SDE generates samples from \(p(x)\): the prior is defined by a neural network.

Diffusion posterior sampling

The score we need vs. the score we have

Sampling the posterior of an inverse problem with the reverse SDE would look like this:

\(\mathrm{d}x_t = \big[f(x_t, t) - g^2(t)\, \underbrace{\nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t \mid y)}_{\text{annealed posterior score}}\big]\mathrm{d}t + g(t)\, \mathrm{d}w\)

Bayes helps…

\(\nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t \mid y) =\)

\(\underbrace{\nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t)}_{\text{denoiser}} + \underbrace{\nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(y \mid x_t)}_{?}\)

…but only half-way

The likelihood of the noisy \(x_t\) is a marginal over all clean \(x\) compatible with it:

\(p_t(y \mid x_t) = \displaystyle\int p(y \mid x)\, p(x \mid x_t)\, \mathrm{d}x\)

\(p(x \mid x_t)\) is intractable in general. Every diffusion posterior sampler is an approximation of this integral, and the quality of that approximation decides the quality of the posterior samples.

Diffusion posterior sampling

The standard approximation: DPS

Chung et al. 2023

Figures from Chung et al. 2023

Replace the intractable marginal by the likelihood evaluated at the denoiser prediction \(\hat{x} = \mathbb{E}[x \mid x_t] \approx d_\theta(x_t, t)\):

\(p_t(y \mid x_t) = \displaystyle\int p(y \mid x)\, p(x \mid x_t)\, \mathrm{d}x \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\big(y \mid \mathbf{A}\hat{x},\, \Sigma_y\big)\)

For a Gaussian likelihood, the posterior score becomes a gradient through the denoiser:

\(\nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t \mid y) \;\approx\; \nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t) \;-\; \nabla_{x_t}\, \tfrac{1}{2}\big(y - \mathbf{A}\hat{x}\big)^\top \Sigma_y^{-1} \big(y - \mathbf{A}\hat{x}\big)\)

Problem: it ignores the posterior uncertainty

DPS treats \(\hat{x}\) as deterministic: it assumes \(p(x \mid x_t)\) concentrates at its mean, which only holds when \(\sigma_t\) is small. The posterior covariance \(\mathbb{V}[x \mid x_t]\) is dropped entirely.

  • Unstable at early (high-noise) diffusion steps
  • Poor coverage of the posterior, samples inconsistent with the observation

Contribution 1 · Moment Matching Posterior Sampling

Keep the covariance: moment matching posterior sampling

Approximate \(p(x \mid x_t)\) by the Gaussian with the right first two moments (closest in KL):

\(q(x \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}\big(x \mid \mathbb{E}[x \mid x_t],\, \mathbb{V}[x \mid x_t]\big)\)

For a linear Gaussian forward model, the marginal is then exact and closed-form:

DPS · wrong

\(\mathcal{N}\big(y \mid \mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}[x \mid x_t],\ \Sigma_y\big)\)

MMPS · less wrong

\(\mathcal{N}\big(y \mid \mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}[x \mid x_t],\ \Sigma_y + {\color{#d9764f}\mathbf{A}\,\mathbb{V}[x \mid x_t]\,\mathbf{A}^\top}\big)\)

Earlier works used heuristics for \(\mathbb{V}[x \mid x_t]\), such as \(\Sigma_t\) or \((\Sigma_t^{-1} + \Sigma_x^{-1})^{-1}\). They ignore the local covariance of the data manifold.

Prior on a 1-d manifold; ellipses: 95% regions of \(q(x \mid x_t)\)

B · with \(\mathbb{V}[x \mid x_t]\): inconsistent regions are correctly pruned

Contribution 1 · Moment Matching Posterior Sampling

Where does the covariance come from? Tweedie, again

For Gaussian corruption \(x_t = x + \epsilon\), \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_t)\), the second-order Tweedie formula gives the posterior covariance from the same denoiser:

\(\boxed{\ \mathbb{V}[x \mid x_t] = \Sigma_t\, \nabla_{x_t} d_\theta(x_t, t)^\top\ }\)

Challenge

The Jacobian \(\nabla_{x_t} d_\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}\) is intractable to materialize in high dimension (\(N \sim 10^5\!-\!10^6\) pixels).

Solution: never build the matrix

The likelihood score only needs the solution of one linear system,

\(\big(\Sigma_y + \mathbf{A}\,\mathbb{V}[x \mid x_t]\,\mathbf{A}^\top\big)\, u = y - \mathbf{A}\hat{x}\)

solved by conjugate gradient using only vector-Jacobian products from automatic differentiation. In practice 1–5 iterations suffice.

Divergence between \(p(x_t \mid y)\) and \(q(x_t \mid y)\) for 1-d manifolds in \(\mathbb{R}^3\):
Tweedie's covariance is orders of magnitude more accurate than heuristics.

Contribution 1 · Moment Matching Posterior Sampling

Fewer steps, better samples

FFHQ 256×256 · MMPS (5) uses 5 conjugate-gradient iterations per step

With the posterior covariance, the sampler is stable from the very first noisy steps: 10 MMPS steps already give consistent reconstructions where DPS needs 1000. Cost: about 16 ms per solver iteration on this task, and 10% more memory than DPS.

Contribution 2 · Learning the prior from observations

A critical assumption we have been making

Assumption so far

We have access to clean training data \(x \sim p(x)\) to learn the diffusion prior.

Reality in scientific applications

We often only have noisy, incomplete observations \(y = \mathbf{A}x + n\), and the corruption may differ for each observation.

  • Astronomy: PSF blur, detector noise, masked or missing pixels, incomplete \(uv\) coverage
  • Medical imaging: sub-sampled MRI \(k\)-space, CT projections
  • Earth sciences: satellite measurements with gaps and noise

Can we learn a diffusion prior directly from corrupted data?

Contribution 2 · Learning the prior from observations

DiEM: diffusion models trained by expectation-maximization

  1. Initialize with a conservative prior \(q_0(x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_x, \Sigma_x)\), itself fitted to the observations by (closed-form) EM.
  2. E-step: for each observation \((y_i, \mathbf{A}_i)\), sample \(x_i \sim q_{\theta_k}(x \mid y_i, \mathbf{A}_i)\) with MMPS.
  3. M-step: train the denoiser \(d_\theta\) on the samples \(\{x_i\}\) with the standard denoising loss, warm-started from \(\theta_k\):

    \(\ell = \lambda_t \lVert d_\theta(x_i + \sigma_t z, t) - x_i\rVert^2\)

  4. Repeat until the prior stops changing (typically 10–30 iterations).

Why MMPS in the E-step?

EM only converges if the posterior samples are accurate. Biased samples give a biased prior, and the bias is amplified at every iteration. With heuristic covariances, the MRI prior collapses after a few rounds.

The M-step is plain diffusion training

No modified loss, any architecture, existing infrastructure. The result is a proper diffusion model, usable for any downstream task.

Physics stays explicit

The forward model \(p(y \mid x, \mathbf{A})\) is never learned: the network only ever sees clean latents it sampled itself.

Accelerated MRI: a prior learned from \(k\)-space measurements only

fastMRI knee scans, acceleration factor \(R\); \(\mathcal{F}^{-1}(m \odot y)\) is the zero-filled baseline

Setup

No clean scan is ever seen. Each observation is a sub-sampled Fourier plane with its own mask \(m\): the forward operator \(\mathbf{A}\) changes from one observation to the next.

Result

Posterior samples are detailed, show plausible variations between draws, and remain consistent with the measured frequencies, even at \(R = 32\).

Part II · Take-home

Physics in the likelihood, data in the prior

Diffusion priors

A learned denoiser is a prior: its score plugs into the reverse SDE, next to an explicit physical likelihood.

MMPS

Keep the posterior covariance, get it from Tweedie's formula, solve with conjugate gradient. Stable, accurate, cheap.

DiEM

Sample the posterior, retrain the prior, repeat. A proper diffusion model from corrupted observations only.

For surveys, this is a recipe for priors learned directly from the data we actually have, with the instrument model kept explicit and never learned.
